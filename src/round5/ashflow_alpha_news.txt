Advisor notes (2 topics):
Orin - Don’t Forget the Fees

Oh, fees. Don’t get me started about those, dear. I have seen so many promising strategies quietly being dismantled by fees. So many. It hurts a little just thinking about them.

See, the thing is: gross opportunity is not net profit. That distinction matters enormously, and yet it is remarkable how often it gets overlooked until it is too late. Have you incorporated all applicable transaction fees into your calculations? Because a strategy that looks attractive before costs can look very different once they are included. Sometimes dramatically so.

Pay particular attention to high-turnover approaches. They tend to accumulate fees in a way that is easy to underestimate when you are focused on the individual trade rather than the aggregate cost.

Ask yourself whether fee structures change when volumes change. If they do, that variation should influence where you allocate capital, not as an afterthought, but as a core part of the calculation. Performance should always be measured after costs, dear. Not just before. Before is just a number. After is the truth.


Orin - Pairing for Profit

Finding a profitable category is satisfying. I will not pretend otherwise. But satisfaction has a way of stopping people exactly where the real work begins. The more important question is never what performed well. It is why.

Do the products within your strongest clusters share measurable similarities? Is there something structural that explains why they move together, or why one tends to follow another with a slight delay? Markets are full of relationships like that, if you know how to look for them. Some products are practically old friends. Did I tell you about my old friend Diego? Diego knows how to spot a cluster. That man has laser eyes! Or he used to anyway. I guess he has lazy eyes now. Aging was not all too easy on his eyesight, I am afraid. Still, you should definitely meet him. Oh, what am I babbling about. We were talking about product relationships. They move together, diverge under specific conditions, and then quietly reconcile again once those conditions pass.

Ask yourself whether the relationships you observe are stable or whether they shift over time. A stable relationship can confirm a signal you were already watching. A shifting one can tell you something has fundamentally changed. Both are useful. Neither should be ignored.

Understanding products in isolation is a start. Understanding how they relate to one another is where conviction comes from.
